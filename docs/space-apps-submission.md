## High-Level Project Summary
SPACE COMMANDER is a bilingual (Arabic/English) web game that turns kids aged 9–12 into the commander of a lunar research
base near the Moon's south pole. Guided by SUHAIL, a friendly robot assistant, players manage power, water, oxygen and food
for four astronauts over seven mission days. Every decision has real, explainable consequences: a power choice on Day 2
changes which water options exist on Day 3 and which emergency plans are possible on Day 5. The base's solar power is
calculated from real NASA LRO/LOLA illumination data for the lunar south pole. At the end, the What If Lab lets children
predict, replay an earlier decision and compare two timelines — learning to think like scientists and engineers.

## Project Details
**What it does**
- Three linked missions: The Vanishing Sun (energy), The Last Drop (water), The Final Signal (space-weather emergency).
- A deterministic simulation engine: each choice changes kWh, litres, oxygen and crop growth; life support is always protected.
- Butterfly effect: earlier choices unlock or lock later options, with SUHAIL explaining why.
- Safety-first design: before the emergency plan, players must complete crew-safety steps.
- Three endings (Mission Accomplished / Safe but Limited / Mission Interrupted) — the crew is always kept safe.
- What If Lab: choose a past decision, choose an alternative, make a prediction, then compare original vs. alternative timelines (charts + table).
- Commander Report: key decisions, cause-and-effect, concepts learned and a transfer question.
- Accessibility: Arabic RTL and English LTR, keyboard navigation, reduced-motion and large-text settings, status shown with words/symbols (not colour only).

**How it works**
Next.js + React + TypeScript web app (static, no accounts, no API keys). Pure-TypeScript simulation and data modules are
separated from the UI and covered by automated tests (16 tests, all endings verified reachable). A Python script
extracts the NASA value reproducibly.

**Benefits**
Children learn that space-habitat systems are interdependent, that trade-offs rarely have one perfect answer, and how to
form and test hypotheses — through play, in their own language.

**Future work**
Final character art and voice narration, more missions and lunar sites, time-varying illumination from NASA SVS
south-pole renderings, classroom/teacher mode.

## Use of Artificial Intelligence
We used an AI coding assistant (Claude Code) to scaffold the Next.js project and to help write the simulation engine,
user interface, tests and documentation from our team's own product brief, story and visual-identity documents.
Team members reviewed and tested the results. No NASA data was generated by AI: the illumination value was extracted
from the official NASA product by a reproducible script and validated. Visual elements in the current build are
temporary vector placeholders made in code; final artwork is by our design team.

## NASA Data
**Lunar Polar Illumination — average solar illumination, 85°S–90°S, 60 m/px (AVGVISIB_85S_060M_201608)**,
NASA Goddard Space Flight Center, Planetary Geodesy Data Archive, product 69 (LRO / LOLA).
- We read the official product and selected the best-lit 60 m pixel within 20 km of Shackleton crater
  (89.475°S, 222.557°E): it is sunlit **88.44 %** of the time (long-term average).
- This value is the input of the game's solar model: `Energy (kWh/day) = 10 kW × 24 h × 0.8844 × 0.85 ≈ 180 kWh`.
  Panel size, efficiency and the story's day-by-day light curve are clearly labelled game assumptions.
- **If the NASA data were removed, the base would have no energy input** — the energy crisis, every decision and every ending depend on it.
- The in-game "About the Science" page shows the source, formula, assumptions and simplifications to players and judges.
